The Suzuki Method

Talent is not born. It is grown, one day at a time.

Dr. Shinichi Suzuki saw that every child learns their native language with ease, and built a way of teaching music on the same idea. Here is what that looks like in your home each week.

01

Listen every day

Students hear their pieces at home until the music feels familiar, long before they play it.

02

A parent beside them

A parent comes to lessons, takes notes, and coaches short daily practice. No musical background needed.

03

Small steps, mastered

Each skill is solid before the next one is added, so confidence grows along with posture and tone.

04

Play for people

Students perform early and often, which turns practice into something worth sharing.

Meet your teacher

Bruce Bowers

Violin · Suzuki Music Academy of Green Bay

Bruce picked up his first violin at three years old. Forty-seven years later, he has spent over two decades helping Green Bay children do the same.

Bruce grew up with the violin in his hands. He has played as concertmaster, assistant concertmaster and principal second violin in local symphony orchestras. He completed his Suzuki teacher training at the Chicago Suzuki Institute and is a registered Suzuki Method teacher through Book 6. In 2004 he joined the Suzuki Music Academy of Green Bay, where he has taught full time ever since.

He has never stopped performing. Audiences across Northeast Wisconsin know him as a soloist at private and community events. Students see a teacher who still plays on real stages, and that shows them where their own practice can lead.

"I don't believe in slow learners. Every student I have taught has learned to play once we found the way they learn best."

Bruce Bowers

That belief is personal. Bruce is a hands-on learner himself, and he knows how it feels when school only teaches one way. So when a student gets stuck, he does not ask them to try harder. He finds another way to explain it. Students in his studio learn that a mistake is information, not failure: you notice it, learn from it, adjust, and play it again.

Parents are part of the team. Bruce coaches families on short, focused daily practice, and he often turns the lesson around so the child teaches the parent what they learned. When a young violinist can show it, say it, and explain why it works, they truly own it.

How Bruce teaches

1The Fix It Count

Students choose one focus, like a straight wrist, and count every time they catch and fix it while playing a review piece. Fixing becomes a game they can win.

2Teach it back

The child teaches the parent at home. Showing and explaining a skill is the fastest way to lock it in.

3Mistakes are information

Try it, notice what happened, learn from it, adjust. Students stop fearing wrong notes and start using them.

4Review every day

Three review pieces a day keep every earlier skill strong, so each new piece rests on a solid foundation.

Questions parents ask

Good to know before you start.

How young can my child start?

Many Suzuki students begin in the preschool years, and Bruce himself started at three. The right age depends on your child. Our teachers will gladly talk it through and suggest a starting point.

Do I need to know how to play?

Not at all. Parents learn right alongside their child. You come to lessons, take notes, and help with short practice at home. Your teacher shows you exactly what to do.

Do we need to buy a violin?

Young students play smaller violins and move up as they grow, so most families rent at first. Your teacher will measure your child and help you find the right size.

Typical ageCommon violin size
3 to 51/16 or 1/10
5 to 71/8 or 1/4
7 to 91/2
9 to 113/4
11 and up4/4, full size
How much practice is expected?

Short and daily beats long and rare. A few focused minutes a day, plus listening to the recordings, adds up fast. Practice time grows naturally as your child grows.

What does tuition cost?

Contact our coordinator for current tuition and lesson lengths. As a nonprofit, our goal is to keep excellent Suzuki teaching within reach for Green Bay families.
